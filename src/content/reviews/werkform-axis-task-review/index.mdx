## What actually adjusts

Most chairs advertise "fully adjustable" and mean height and tilt. The Axis adjusts
seat height, seat depth, backrest tension, backrest angle, lumbar height, lumbar
depth, and armrest height, width, depth, and angle. That is ten independent
adjustments, and the two that matter most — seat depth and lumbar height — are the
two most commonly missing at this price.

Seat depth is the one people underestimate. A seat pan that is too long for your
femurs puts pressure behind the knee and pulls you forward out of the lumbar
support, and no amount of backrest adjustment fixes it. The Axis has 60 mm of
travel, which covers roughly the 5th to 95th percentile of adult leg length.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Set it in this order">
  Seat height first, so your feet are flat and your thighs are level. Then seat depth, until there
  are two fingers between the seat edge and the back of your knee. Then lumbar height, to sit in the
  small of your back. Armrests last, and lower than you think. Most people never get past step one.
</Callout>

## Eight weeks in

The measurable claim about a chair is whether it stays where you put it. We marked
every adjustment point at setup and re-measured at four and eight weeks. Nothing had
moved: no gas lift sink, no backrest tension drift, no armrest creep. That sounds
like a low bar and it is one that three of the last six chairs we have tested failed.

Subjectively, the thing the Axis does that cheaper chairs do not is stay comfortable
through the fourth and fifth hour. The seat foam is firm — genuinely uncomfortable
for the first week, in the way a good mattress is — and after a fortnight it stops
registering at all. Both testers reported the same arc.

## The assembly

Three boxes. Twenty-two components. A diagram with no text, in which the armrest
brackets are drawn from an angle that makes left and right indistinguishable. We
fitted them backwards, ran the chair for two days wondering why the armrest travel
felt wrong, and took them off again.

Fifty-eight minutes the first time, thirty-four the second, and the second was with
knowledge no first-time buyer will have. Werkform has clearly never had to think
about this, because contract customers pay someone else to do it.

<Callout tone="caution" title="The armrest brackets are handed">
  The stamped part number faces outward. This is not in the instructions, and the brackets will bolt
  on either way round.
</Callout>

## The warranty, which is the actual product

Twelve years on every part, and — more usefully — a parts store that sells casters
for $9, a gas lift for $45, and armrest pads for $22. A chair you can keep repairing
is a different purchase from a chair you replace, and Werkform's record on honouring
the warranty is the best of any brand we track.

At $899 over twelve years, this is $75 a year. The $300 chair you replace every
three years is $100 a year and worse to sit in.

## The verdict

Buy it, block out an hour, and read the diagram twice before you touch a bolt.
