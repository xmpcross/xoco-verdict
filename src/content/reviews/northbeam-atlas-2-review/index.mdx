Northbeam has been making speakers since 1994 and headphones since 2019, and until
now you could hear the difference. The first Atlas was an excellent transducer
bolted to a headband that seemed to have been designed from a photograph of a head
rather than a measurement of one. It won arguments and lost commutes.

The Atlas II keeps the arguments.

## What the cancellation actually does

Noise cancellation is easy to describe badly. Manufacturers quote a single number —
usually the deepest attenuation their system reaches at the one frequency it is best
at — and that number tells you almost nothing about a train carriage.

We measure across the band on a fixed head-and-torso rig, three runs, pads reseated
between each. The figure that matters for transport is the one at 100 Hz, because
that is where engine and rail rumble lives, and it is where cheap systems fall over.

The pattern is consistent with what Northbeam publishes, which is worth saying out
loud: the company's own numbers matched ours to within 1.2 dB at every point we
measured. That is not universal.

The one place the Atlas II gives ground is high treble, where passive isolation does
most of the work and the pad seal matters more than the electronics. If your problem
is a colleague two desks away rather than a diesel engine, the gap between this and
a cheaper pair narrows considerably.

<Callout tone="method" title="Why we never quote a single dB figure">
  A cancellation system is a filter, and a filter has a shape. Averaging that shape into one number
  lets a manufacturer optimise for the average instead of for the noise you are actually sitting in.
  Every cancellation figure on this site is quoted at a stated frequency for that reason.
</Callout>

## The tuning

Neutral, with about 2 dB of lift below 80 Hz and a very slight recession around
3 kHz that takes the edge off compressed pop without dulling anything.

This is not exciting. Played against the Drift Pro, which lifts the low bass harder
and pushes the presence region forward, the Atlas II sounds flat for about ninety
seconds and then sounds correct for the rest of the day. That is the trade Northbeam
has always made, and it remains the right one for anything you listen to for hours.

Two specific notes from listening. First, the cancellation does not audibly change
the frequency response when switched on — a common failing, and one that makes a
headphone useless for critical work. Second, at maximum volume there is no
compression or distortion we could hear or measure, which suggests the amplifier has
more headroom than the drivers need.

## Living with it

The headband is the story. Clamp force is down from 6.1 N to 4.4 N, the pad opening
is 8 mm taller, and the weight has dropped by 45 g. In practice that is the
difference between taking them off after two hours and forgetting they are on.

The cost is the folding mechanism, which has been deleted. The Atlas II lays flat and
that is all. Northbeam's engineering note says the hinge was the most common warranty
claim on the original, which is a defensible reason to remove it, but the supplied
case is 24 cm across and will not go into a bag that is already carrying a laptop.

<Callout tone="caution" title="Check the firmware before judging transparency mode">
  Units shipped before 2.1.4 have an audible noise floor in transparency. The update reduces it
  considerably but does not eliminate it. Ours arrived on 2.1.2.
</Callout>

## Against the alternatives

Inside the Northbeam catalogue the useful comparison is not the Cadence One —
different product, different room — but the original Atlas, still being sold at a
discount. Do not buy it. The measurements are close and the wearing experience is
not.

Against the Halcyon Drift Pro, the question is form factor before anything else. The
Drift Pro isolates about 7 dB less at 100 Hz and sounds livelier; it also fits in a
pocket, which the Atlas II emphatically does not.

## The verdict

At $349 this is the pair to buy if you want the noise to go away and the music to
stay honest. The case is a genuine annoyance and the app is a waste of a download.
Neither is a reason to buy something that isolates 7 dB worse.
