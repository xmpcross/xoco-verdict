Until last year every guide on this site had three picks: the best one, the value
one, and the budget one. It is a good structure. It gives every reader something,
and the budget pick is always the most-clicked link on the page.

Then we went back and looked at what happened to the budget picks.

## What we found

We contacted 140 readers who had bought a budget pick from one of our guides between
2023 and 2025, and asked one question: do you still have it?

Three fifths of the people who followed our budget recommendation had replaced it,
and the median amount they had spent by the end was nearly twice the price of the
pick we had put at the top of the page.

We had, with good intentions and accurate testing, recommended the more expensive
option.

## Cost per year is the number nobody prints

A $145 running shoe that lasts 400 miles costs 36 cents a mile. A $110 shoe that
lasts 220 costs 50 cents a mile. The cheaper shoe is 39% more expensive.

A $899 chair with a twelve-year warranty and $9 casters costs $75 a year. A $300
chair replaced every three years costs $100 a year, and you sit in a worse chair for
all twelve of them.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is not an argument for buying the most expensive thing">
  Above a certain point extra money buys materials and finish rather than lifespan, and the
  cost-per-year curve flattens and then reverses. The argument is for finding where that curve
  bottoms out in a category, which is usually well below the flagship and well above the cheapest
  thing that works.
</Callout>

## Repairability moves the number more than quality does

The single biggest factor in whether a product was still owned after two years was
not how well it was built. It was whether a consumable part could be replaced.

Headphones with replaceable earpads survived. Headphones with glued pads were thrown
away when the pads flaked, which is a two-year event regardless of price. Grinders
with user-replaceable burrs survived; sealed ones did not. A $15 bellows extends the
useful life of a Grind X by years, and a $22 armrest pad does the same for a chair.

This is the thing to look at on a specification sheet, and it is almost never on one.
Search the manufacturer's site for a parts store instead. If there is not one, assume
the product is disposable and price it accordingly.

## What we do now

We still recommend inexpensive products, and several of our current best-value picks
cost less than half of the top pick in their guide. What changed is that we no longer
fill the slot out of obligation.

If nothing under a certain price measured well enough this round, the guide says
that. Our current headphone guide has no budget pick for exactly that reason, and we
would rather publish a two-pick guide than send a reader to something we expect them
to replace.

## What to ask before you buy the cheap one

- How long does the consumable part last, and can I replace it myself?
- Is there a parts store, with prices, on the manufacturer's site?
- What is the warranty, and is there any record of it being honoured?
- What is the price divided by the number of years I expect to keep it?

If the last number is higher than the same calculation for something dearer, buy the
dearer one. That is the whole argument.
