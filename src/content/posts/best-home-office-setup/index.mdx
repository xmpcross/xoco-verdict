## The order

Chair, then desk, then light. Almost everybody does this backwards, because a desk is
the thing you can see and a chair is the thing you sit in.

You spend six or more hours a day in contact with the chair and roughly none in
contact with the desk. A good chair on a cheap desk is a good working day. A good
desk with a bad chair is a back problem with a nice surface on top of it.

The lamp comes last and is the cheapest of the three, but it is the one people are
most surprised by. Bad light is why your eyes ache at four o'clock, and a reflection
in the middle of a glossy monitor is a problem you stop noticing and never stop
being affected by.

## Set the chair up properly, once

The Axis has ten adjustments and most people will use two. That is the same mistake
as buying a cheaper chair.

Seat height first, until your feet are flat and your thighs are level. Then seat
depth, until there are two fingers between the front edge and the back of your knee
— this is the adjustment almost nobody makes and the one that stops you sliding
forward out of the lumbar support. Then lumbar height, into the small of your back.
Armrests last, and lower than feels natural, so your shoulders drop.

It takes ten minutes and it is worth more than the difference between a $400 chair
and a $900 one.

## What to ignore when you shop

**"Ergonomic".** The word is unregulated and appears on office chairs with fixed
armrests and no lumbar adjustment. Look at the adjustment list instead.

**Desk motor count without a stability figure.** Dual motors lift more smoothly.
They do not stop a frame flexing. Ask for deflection at height, or push the display
model hard at the edge.

**Lumens on a desk lamp.** A very bright lamp with poor colour rendering and a
symmetrical beam is worse than a dimmer one with 96 CRI and an asymmetric throw.

**Warranty length without a parts store.** A twelve-year warranty is only useful if
the company will sell you a $9 caster in year seven rather than making it a claim.
