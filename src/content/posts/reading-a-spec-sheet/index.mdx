Specification sheets are written by marketing departments working from engineering
data, and the gap between those two documents is where most bad purchases live.
Nothing on a spec sheet is usually false. Plenty of it is irrelevant, and the
relevant parts are frequently absent.

Here are the six questions that separate the two, in any category.

## 1. Is it a peak or a sustained figure?

A processor's boost clock is a peak. A monitor's peak brightness is measured on a
small window, not the whole panel. A grinder's throughput is measured with the
hopper full.

Peaks describe the best moment a product has. Sustained figures describe what it does
for the length of the task, and they are almost never published — which is why we
measure clock speed at thirty minutes rather than at ten seconds, and why the
Meridian 14 loses on the specification sheet and wins in use.

<Callout tone="tip" title="The tell">
  If a figure is a single number with no duration attached, assume it is a peak. If the marketing
  copy says "bursts of" or "up to", it definitely is.
</Callout>

## 2. Under what condition?

"Eleven hours of battery life" is not a claim until you know the screen brightness,
the workload, and whether the wireless was on.

Battery figures move by 40% or more between 120 nits and 300 nits. Headphone battery
figures move by a third between 65 dB SPL and 80. Every number we publish carries
its condition for that reason, and any number that does not carry one is a number
measured under whatever condition produced it.

## 3. What is the tolerance?

This is the question almost nobody asks, and in several categories it matters more
than the value itself.

An espresso machine set to 93 C that varies by 2 C between shots produces
inconsistent coffee. One that sits at 91 C and varies by 0.4 C produces consistent
coffee you can dial in. The second machine has the worse specification and the better
behaviour.

The same logic applies to grind distribution, panel uniformity, GPS drift, and shoe
sizing. Consistency is the thing you experience; the headline value is the thing you
can adjust.

## 4. Is the missing number the important one?

Manufacturers publish specifications that flatter them. The absence of a figure is
therefore information.

No published sustained clock speed means the sustained clock speed is not good. No
published retention figure on a grinder means retention is high. No published
firmware support window means there is not one. No stated frequency for a noise
cancellation figure means it was measured where the product is strongest.

<Callout tone="caution" title="A useful habit">
  Before buying, list the three numbers you would most want to know. Then check which of them the
  manufacturer publishes. The ones missing are usually the ones that would have changed your mind.
</Callout>

## 5. Is it measured or derived?

Some figures are measured on the product. Others are calculated from component
specifications and never verified.

Rated stabilisation stops, CIPA battery ratings, and "equivalent" measurements of any
kind are derived. They are useful for comparing products within one manufacturer's
range and close to useless across manufacturers, because everyone derives
differently.

The R7's CIPA rating is 580 frames. We got 1,100 in normal shooting. Neither number
is wrong; they are answers to different questions, and only one of them is your
question.

## 6. Does the specification describe a part or a product?

A monitor with a good panel can have a bad scaler. A laptop with a fast processor can
have a cooling system that never lets it run. A chair with a mesh backrest that
appears in every premium chair can have a frame that flexes.

Component specifications tell you what is possible. They do not tell you what the
manufacturer did with it, and the difference between a $649 monitor and a $1,200 one
with the same panel is entirely in that gap.

## The short version

Ask for the duration, the condition, and the tolerance. Notice what is missing. Then
find someone who measured it.
